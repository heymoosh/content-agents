Every strike against Hawai'i's Big Five plantation companies failed for the same reason. They were organized along single-ethnic lines, so the companies used one group as strikebreakers against another. Five interlocking companies controlled more than 100,000 workers by keeping them separated: Paid differently, housed separately, pitted against one another.

The 1946 strike broke that pattern. The breakthrough was a new relationship, not a new tactic.

The ILWU required leadership at every level to span ethnic groups. Meetings ran in multiple languages. Shared work assignments made solidarity a daily practice instead of an abstract ideal.

When 26,000 workers walked out, the companies imported 6,000 Filipino workers as replacements. Filipino union members met them at the docks. The replacements stepped off the ships wearing union buttons.

What held 76,000 people together for 79 days was infrastructure. Fishing crews, hunting teams, communal food kitchens, a transportation network, community safety patrols, backyard gardening collectives, alternative banking, daily coordination across four islands. The goal was simple: No one goes hungry, no one is isolated, no one has to choose between the strike and feeding their family.

That infrastructure was also the attack. The companies' coercive leverage depended entirely on workers' material dependency: Company housing, company medical care, company stores. Building alternative systems of support sustained the strike and broke the dependency that made the plantation system possible.

I keep coming back to this when people ask what ordinary power actually looks like. Power requires leverage, and the cost of ignoring that pressure has to be stronger than the incentives that keep things the way they are.

The forms I see most clearly are our votes, our labor, our spending, our care for one another, and our ability to build new tools and institutions.
