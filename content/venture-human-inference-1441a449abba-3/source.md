Every previous plantation strike in Hawai‘i had failed.

Workers were divided by ethnicity, paid differently, housed separately, and used as strikebreakers against one another.

The breakthrough wasn’t a better protest tactic. It was the relationships workers had built.

The ILWU organized across ethnic groups and languages. During the 1946 strike, people created communal kitchens, fishing crews, transportation, safety patrols, gardens, dances, and alternative banking.

When replacement workers arrived from the Philippines, union members met them at the docks.

They stepped off the ships wearing union buttons.

The systems of care did more than sustain the strike. They broke the dependency plantation owners had used to control people.

I’m not organizing a strike, but I ran into a much smaller version of this while facilitating a democracy group. Trying to get thirteen people with different ideas to agree on one big project produced very little. Looking for two people with something useful to offer each other was much easier.

If a project you care about is missing a skill, open your contacts and find one person who might have it. Send them this:

“I’m working on [specific thing], and I think your experience with [specific skill] could help. Would you have twenty minutes to help me think through one next step?”

You don’t need to assemble a movement. You can start with one useful conversation.

The Hawai‘i story and the research behind it are in [The world’s broken. What do we do?](https://www.humaninference.ai/essays/the-worlds-broken-what-do-we-do). I’m collecting more of what I learn about collective action on my [Actions page](https://www.humaninference.ai/actions).
