> Recently the product community lost one of its most influential and impactful pioneers, Frank Robinson.
>
> Most product people have unfortunately never heard of Frank, because he preferred hands-on coaching and advising over self-promotion.
>
> However, he was the person that coined the term "Minimum Viable Product" way back in 2001, and created a modern approach to product development ​initially called "Synchronous Development," and​ later called "Market Validation," ​and what we now call ​"Customer Development.​"
>
> Much of my work, as well as that of Eric Ries, Steve Blank, and likely many others was heavily influenced by Frank's work.
>
> Very early on Frank realized the deficiencies of the project model and the old waterfall approach. In particular, he saw that value risk (would people actually buy this product?) was clearly the most important risk, yet in the project model, this risk was essentially ignored until after the product had been built, resulting in countless failed products.
>
> Frank believed a product team should focus on value first and foremost, and they could discover customers with real unmet needs in parallel with discovering the product which would meet those needs, and that a cross-functional team could converge much more quickly on what we now call product/market fit.
>
> ​Frank deeply understood the distinction between discovery and delivery, and was famous for drilling into product teams his mantra: "Nail it, then scale it."
>
> He built a product coaching business that helped hundreds of mostly B2B companies to discover and deliver winning products. Many of the people he coached went on to start their own companies, and over two decades influenced companies far beyond Silicon Valley.
>
> In 2017, Jeff Patton offered to introduce me, and I was fortunate enough to finally meet, and share stories and experiences over a dinner. I was also happy to meet his daughter, Alexa Robinson, who has followed in her father's footsteps and pursued the product path.
>
> I remember trying to encourage Frank to write a book to share what he had learned, and while he did have ​a​ draft describing his Market Validation method, I'm sorry to say he never published.
>
> But the success of Frank's coaching and his methods has left a considerable impact on our industry, even if most people are unaware of the origin story.
