> **1. Dementia is NOT inevitable.**
>
> Up to 45% of dementia cases may be preventable by addressing known risk factors across the lifespan. 45%. Nearly half. People used to think cognitive decline was just what happened. It is not. Genetics matter. But habits, health, and lifestyle matter too, often more than people realize. You are not powerless here.
>
> **2. Depression after 50 does not always look like sadness.**
>
> For many people in their fifties, sixties, and beyond, depression shows up as memory loss, brain fog, cognitive slowing, difficulty finding words, and withdrawal from people and activities you once loved.
>
> It is so convincing it has its own name: pseudodementia. Real, extremely common, and completely reversible when the depression is treated. It is quite common for someone to come see me at 60 convinced they are developing dementia, and within 40 minutes I am abundantly certain they are not. They have pseudodementia. And we treat it. Months later they feel like themselves again.
>
> **3. The sleeping pill you are afraid to take is often safer for your brain than what you are already taking.**
>
> The ones I worry about most: Tylenol PM, ZzzQuil, Benadryl, Ambien, Lunesta, Xanax, to name a few.
>
> The active ingredient in most over the counter PM sleep products is diphenhydramine (which is the generic form of Benadryl). It blocks acetylcholine, which is critical for memory and learning, and is the same system that breaks down in Alzheimer's disease. Chronic daily use has been associated with an increased risk of dementia. And the sleep it produces is not great quality.
>
> Ambien, Lunesta, and Xanax are in a category of their own. They may have a place for short term relief, but long term use is a different story. Increased fall risk, dependence, withdrawal, and increased risk of cognitive decline, to name a few concerns.
>
> There are safer paths forward. Most people just do not know about them.
>
> **4. Hearing loss and vision loss are two of the most underrated dementia risk factors we have.**
>
> When hearing declines, the brain works harder just to process sound. That increased cognitive load accelerates decline over time. The same is true for vision loss.
>
> The 2024 Lancet Commission on Dementia lists both as significant modifiable risk factors.
>
> When did you last have your hearing tested? Your eyes checked? These are brain health appointments.
>
> **5. Your thyroid could be behind your brain fog, memory problems, and low mood.**
>
> Hypothyroidism produces a remarkably convincing picture of cognitive decline. Slowed thinking, fatigue, memory problems, depression.
>
> I have had multiple patients come to me convinced they had early Alzheimer's. A simple blood test revealed untreated hypothyroidism. After treatment, their symptoms resolved.
>
> A TSH and Free T4 test can catch it quickly. If you have not had your thyroid checked recently, ask your doctor.
>
> **6. Vitamin B12 deficiency can look exactly like early dementia.**
>
> Low B12 can cause confusion, memory problems, mood changes, and cognitive slowing that closely mimic both depression and dementia. One of the most important reversible causes of cognitive decline, and it gets missed constantly. When caught early, it is reversible.
>
> **7. Less than six hours of sleep per night is one of the fastest ways to accelerate cognitive decline.**
>
> Sleep is when your brain consolidates memories, clears metabolic waste, and restores itself. Chronic deprivation impairs attention, memory, emotional regulation, and has been linked to increased dementia risk over time.
>
> Month after month, year after year of insufficient sleep gets normalized as just how things are. It does not have to be that way. Sleep disorders are treatable.

> **9. Blood sugar is quietly one of the biggest threats to your aging brain.**
>
> Chronically elevated blood sugar can damage blood vessels in your brain and promote inflammation. Diabetes significantly increases dementia risk. An A1C test gives you a three month snapshot of your average blood sugar. Ask your doctor about it.
>
> **10. Chronic stress can physically change your brain.**
>
> Prolonged elevated cortisol can negatively affect the hippocampus, the part of your brain most responsible for memory and learning. Not just feeling stressed. Measurable structural changes over time. Managing stress is literally brain preservation.
>
> **11. The APOE4 gene raises your Alzheimer's risk — but it does not determine your fate.**
>
> APOE4 is a gene variant that significantly increases the risk of developing Alzheimer's disease. You can test for it through a blood test. One copy raises risk meaningfully. Two copies raise it more.
>
> But many people with two copies of APOE4 never develop Alzheimer's. And many people without it do. Genetics set the stage, but lifestyle shapes the performance.
>
> I go back and forth on the utility of testing for APOE4 for Alzheimer's. It is expensive for one thing. And it raises an honest question — do you want to know if you are at increased risk if you are already doing everything you can? I have seen patients come to me deeply depressed after testing positive. Personally, I have not tested myself despite a family history of Alzheimer's. I am doing everything I can regardless. I always have an honest conversation with patients about it and support whatever they decide.
>
> **12. When did you last get your blood work done?**
>
> I nearly pass out every time I get a blood draw. I hate doing it. But it is essential. Seriously, when was the last time you had your blood pressure checked? Your labs drawn?
>
> For brain health specifically, I am thinking CBC, A1C, TSH, vitamin B12, and vitamin D to start. Paid subscribers get my full reversible causes of dementia bloodwork panel at the bottom of this newsletter.

>
> **13. Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) is not a death sentence.**
>
> MCI sits between normal aging and dementia. Many people with MCI never progress. Some improve. This is the critical intervention window, when lifestyle changes, treating reversible contributors, and proper monitoring can make a meaningful difference.
>
> **14. Resistance training is just as important as cardio for your brain.**
>
> Resistance training preserves muscle mass, supports bone density, reduces fall risk, and improves metabolic health in ways that directly protect cognitive function. Two sessions per week is enough. You do not need to become a bodybuilder. Just lift something heavy on a regular basis.
>
> **15. Many people who are afraid they have dementia do not have dementia.**
>
> Many people who come to see me convinced they are developing Alzheimer's are actually dealing with depression, anxiety, undiagnosed ADHD, sleep disorders, thyroid dysfunction, medication side effects, burnout, or hormonal changes.

> When people find out that I am a psychiatrist who also specializes in helping people sleep better, I sometimes hear something like this:
>
> "I would never take a sleeping pill. I don't want to put that kind of thing in my body."
>

>

>
> Group 3: They just suffer. Four to five hours of sleep a night (or less), sometimes for months or years, refusing any kind of help because they are afraid of what taking something might do to their brain.
>
> Here is the uncomfortable truth that I want to share with all three groups.
>
> The medication many people are avoiding out of fear? In most cases, they are far safer for your brain long term than the over the counter options they are taking every night without a second thought
>
> And in my opinion, both of those are safer than chronic untreated insomnia. Because less than five hours of sleep per night, month after month, year after year, does real and measurable damage to the brain.
>
> This disconnect is something I think about constantly. And today I want to clear it up.
>

>

>
> Okay. Back to your brain.
>
> **A Link Is Not A Cause**
>
> Before we get into specific medications, something important.
>
> Over the years there have been headlines about lots of different medication being linked to dementia. And I understand why those headlines are alarming.
>
> But one study showing a link between a medication and a bad outcome does not change practice (usually). What we look for is consistent findings across many large, high quality studies and meta-analyses — research that combines and compares dozens or hundreds of studies over periods of time to ask whether there is actually a causal relationship.
>
> The medications I am about to discuss? There is a substantial body of evidence behind the concerns I am raising. Not one headline. Not one observational study. Consistent data, over time, across populations.
>
> And not only do I see it in the research — I see it in my memory clinic as well.
>
> **The Medications I Am Genuinely Cautious About Long Term**
>
> Diphenhydramine — the ingredient hiding in plain sight
>
> Diphenhydramine is the active ingredient in Benadryl. It was originally developed as an antihistamine — meaning it blocks histamine receptors to reduce allergy symptoms like sneezing, itching, and runny nose. And it's very effective for this use.
>
> But it has a side effect that the sleep aid industry figured out a long time ago. Because it crosses into the brain and blocks histamine there too, it makes you drowsy. So the same ingredient that treats your allergies got repackaged as a sleep aid and put into so many over the counter sleep products on the market.
>
> Here is the problem.
>
> Diphenhydramine also blocks a brain chemical called acetylcholine. Acetylcholine is critical for memory, learning, and cognitive function. It is actually the same system that breaks down in Alzheimer's disease — which is why Alzheimer's medications work by trying to preserve acetylcholine.
>
> When you take diphenhydramine occasionally for allergies or the odd sleepless night, the risk is likely minimal.
>
> But taking diphenhydramine every night for months or years? The evidence for long term cognitive harm is significant and consistent. A major study published in JAMA Internal Medicine reported a roughly 50% increased risk for dementia among adults over 65 who used anticholinergics (like diphenhydramine) daily for over three years.
>
> And on top of the cognitive risk, the sleep you get from diphenhydramine is not actually good quality sleep. It sedates you, but it does not produce the deep, restorative sleep stages your brain needs to consolidate memory and clear metabolic waste.
>
> You are sedated. Not truly rested.
>
> When patients come to me using these products nightly, my goal is to transition them to something that is actually safe, well tolerated, and produces real sleep. More on that below.
>
> Benzodiazepines — Xanax, Ativan, Klonopin, Valium, etc
>
> These medications are valuable in the right context. A panic attack. Severe acute anxiety. Alcohol withdrawal. Flight anxiety. A medical procedure. Short term situational stress.
>
> The problem is long term daily use — especially in adults over 50.
>
> Chronic benzodiazepine use is associated with cognitive fog, increased fall risk, tolerance, and dependence. Falls in older adults are not minor events. They lead to fractures, hospitalizations, immobility, and in some cases a rapid cognitive decline that never fully reverses.
>
> I help patients taper off benzodiazepines daily in my memory clinic. And I want to be extremely clear about something:
>
> I do not ever recommend stopping these medications on your own.
>
> Benzodiazepine withdrawal is not only uncomfortable. It can be dangerous. It can cause severe rebound anxiety, insomnia, and seizures. In some cases it can be life threatening.
>
> The way I approach this with my patients is a slow, carefully supervised taper — often over months depending on the dose and duration of use. I also work with compounding pharmacies in many cases, which allows me to reduce the dose in increments so small that patients often barely feel the transition.
>
> The goal is to bring someone off the medication so gradually that their nervous system barely registers the change.
>
> Done correctly, the cognitive clarity people experience on the other side is one of the most rewarding transformations I get to witness.
>
> Z-drugs — Ambien, Lunesta
>
> More targeted than benzodiazepines but carry similar concerns with chronic use. Tolerance, dependence, fall risk, and emerging data on possible long term cognitive effects. Helpful short term. I do prescribe them for short term use occasionally. Problematic when they become a nightly habit for years.
>
> I help patients taper off these as well, using the same careful, supervised approach.
>
> Gabapentin
>
> This one is controversial. It has become increasingly prescribed for nerve pain, anxiety, and sleep. Can be genuinely helpful in the right context. But at higher doses over time, gabapentin can cause sedation and brain fog. There is emerging data suggesting a possible link with mild cognitive decline — though I want to be honest that the evidence here is still early and evolving and I don't have a clear answer here. I'm following the research and will let you all know when the situation is more conclusive.
>
> **What To Do If You Are Taking One of These Medications**
>
> If you are taking a nightly over the counter PM product, look at the ingredient label. If you see diphenhydramine, that is worth a conversation with your doctor about safer alternatives.
>
> If you have been on a benzodiazepine or Ambien for years and have noticed changes in your thinking or memory — that connection may be real. And there are safer paths forward, done carefully and with proper support.
>
> One of the things I do in every initial evaluation at my memory clinic is a full medication and supplement reconciliation. We go through everything together — every prescription, every over the counter product, every supplement — and I advise on what may be contributing to cognitive symptoms and what safer alternatives exist.
>
> This is not just a conversation about what to stop. It is a conversation about your full cognitive picture — the risks you may not know you are carrying, and a personalized plan to address them.
>
> If you would like to have a conversation about working together, you can schedule a free call with me here. No pressure — just an honest discussion about your situation and whether I can help.
>
> **The Medications That Are Unfairly Blamed**
>
> This part matters just as much. Because fear of medication keeps a lot of people from getting treatment they genuinely need. And untreated mental health conditions carry their own very real cognitive risks.
>
> Antidepressants — SSRIs and SNRIs
>
> Sertraline. Escitalopram. Duloxetine. Venlafaxine. Etc.
>
> These are among the most studied medications in all of psychiatry. And the consistent finding across many large, high quality trials is that they tend to be safe for cognitive health for long term use. Are they side effect free? Nope. I wish. Common side effects include GI distress, reduced libido, and a few others, but they do not appear to increase risk of dementia.
>
> You know what is not safe for cognitive health? Untreated, severe depression — a condition that itself significantly increases dementia risk.
>
> Untreated depression is far more damaging to the brain than the medications used to treat it. I recently wrote an entire newsletter about pseudodementia — depression that causes cognitive symptoms so convincing they get mistaken for Alzheimer's. When the depression is treated, those symptoms often resolve completely.
>
> Antidepressants, used appropriately, protect the brain. They do not harm it.

> **The woman with the wire in her brain**
>
> Here is something that actually happened.
>
> A woman in her sixties lay on an operating table with an electrode threaded deep into her nucleus accumbens, the small knot of tissue that helps the brain decide what feels rewarding. She had lived with severe obesity and loss-of-control eating for years. While she watched pictures of food, the electrode picked up bursts of electrical activity that rose and fell with her cravings. You could see the wanting on a screen.
>
> Then the researchers did something simple. They gave her a weekly injection of tirzepatide, the dual GLP-1/GIP drug many people know as Mounjaro or Zepbound.
>
> Over the weeks that followed, the abnormal craving signals got smaller. Her weight dropped. The constant, intrusive thoughts about food eased. The drive in her brain did not vanish, but it quieted in a way the electrode could measure and she could feel.
>
> A gut hormone had changed the way her reward circuit behaved. And that single patient, with a wire in her brain and a needle in her thigh, tells you almost everything you need to know about where this story is headed.
>
> **The puzzle that started with a glass of sugar water**
>
> In the 1960s, researchers ran a beautifully simple experiment. They gave people glucose two ways: by mouth, and straight into a vein. Same amount, same sugar. On paper the body should respond identically.
>
> It didn't. When people drank the glucose, their pancreases released significantly more insulin. Something in the gut was sending a message the vein couldn't replicate.
>
> It took decades to identify the messengers. First came GIP, a hormone from the upper small intestine that surges when fat and carbohydrates arrive. Then GLP-1, from the lower gut, which boosts insulin, slows stomach emptying, and, this turned out to be the important part, acts directly on brain regions that control hunger and reward.
>
> The trouble was that natural GLP-1 vanishes from the blood in about two minutes. Building a version that lasted became one of the great puzzles of modern pharmacology. Part of the answer came from an unlikely place: the saliva of the Gila monster, a desert lizard, which carried a peptide that looked a lot like GLP-1 but didn't break down nearly as fast. Scientists eventually engineered once-weekly injectables, semaglutide, tirzepatide, that produced average weight loss of fifteen to twenty percent or more in clinical trials. Numbers that placed them next to bariatric surgery on the chart.
>
> All of it started with a glass of sugar water and a question nobody could answer.
>
> **What changes when the drug is on board**
>
> The clinical picture is striking. For people with obesity, high-dose semaglutide produces roughly fifteen percent average weight loss over sixteen months. Tirzepatide exceeds twenty percent. For type 2 diabetes, A1c drops of one to two-plus percentage points are common, with many patients reaching normal range.
>
> But the numbers that matter most aren't about the scale. Weight loss in the ten-to-twenty percent range is linked to diabetes remission, improvement in fatty liver, less sleep apnea, lower blood pressure, and, in large cardiovascular trials, fewer heart attacks, strokes, and cardiovascular deaths. When these drugs work, they don't just make people lighter. They make the whole metabolic picture better at once.
>
> And then there is the thing patients talk about most: the quiet.
>
> **The volume knob**
>
> If you have never experienced what patients call "food noise," it is hard to explain. It is a constant mental chatter about eating. "What can I get next?" "Did I bring snacks?" "Maybe I should stop for a drive-through." The thoughts arrive even when the person is not physically hungry. They are intrusive and relentless and, for many people, exhausting.
>
> Brain imaging helps explain why. In people with obesity, reward regions light up intensely in response to food cues, a bag of chips on a counter, the smell of frying oil in an airport. The higher those signals, the harder it is to say no. GLP-1 drugs turn the volume down. People report less hunger between meals, fewer cravings for processed foods, and less pull from visual cues. In lab tasks they choose smaller portions without being told to. The neurosurgical case above measured that change with electrodes.
>
> In plain terms: when the internal drive is softer, people can finally make use of nutrition advice that felt impossible before. The advice didn't fail them. The volume was just too loud to hear it.
>
> **What you trade for the quiet**
>
> Nothing this powerful is free. The most common side effects are gastrointestinal, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, especially early on. Most cases are mild to moderate and ease with time. Slow dose titration helps. Rarer but serious risks include pancreatitis, gallbladder disease (likely from rapid weight loss itself), and a thyroid cancer signal that appeared in rodents but has not been seen in humans.
>
> Then there is the muscle question. Social media will tell you these drugs "melt your muscle." The data tells a more boring and more useful story. About seventy to eighty percent of the weight lost is fat. Twenty to thirty percent is lean mass, roughly the same ratio as diet-only weight loss or bariatric surgery. The percentage of body weight that is lean often goes up, because fat drops more. The real concern is in older or already frail adults. The fix is not mysterious: strength training at least twice a week, adequate protein, and not starving yourself. We will come back to that.
>
> **The cliff and the runway**
>
> Here is where the story gets uncomfortable.
>
> In a major semaglutide trial, participants who stopped the drug after sixty-eight weeks regained about two-thirds of their lost weight within a year. A tirzepatide withdrawal study showed a similar pattern. The biology is straightforward: when weight drops, the body raises hunger hormones, lowers fullness hormones, and reduces energy expenditure. The drug was holding all of those forces in check. Remove it, and the old biology is still there, plus a stronger drive to regain.
>
> That is the cliff. But there is also evidence of a runway.
>
> In a large digital clinic program, patients who tapered semaglutide slowly over several weeks, with close nutrition and exercise coaching, held weight steady for at least six months after fully stopping. On average, they even lost a bit more during the taper itself. It is not a guarantee. But it is a hint that a planned landing beats jumping off a cliff.
>
> **The better question**
>
> Most people are asking "Can I come off?"
>
> The more useful question is: "What has to be in place so that stopping doesn't undo my health?"
>
> The answer is what you might call a lifestyle platform, a set of habits built while you are still on the medication, strong enough to carry you when appetite comes back online. Think of the drug as scaffolding. The platform is the building. You do not take the scaffolding down until the building can stand on its own.
>
> These are the six habits that need to be in place before you consider tapering:
>
> • Protein at every meal. Two to three meals a day with a real protein anchor, 25 to 35 grams per meal. Do not let the medication turn you into a "tiny bites" eater. That is how muscle loss and fatigue creep in.
>
> • Strength training, consistently. At minimum twice a week, full-body resistance work. It does not have to be fancy. It has to happen. This is the loudest signal your body gets to hold on to muscle.
>
> • Fiber that feeds the system. Aim for 25 to 40 grams a day, vegetables, beans, lentils, fruit. Fiber is part of the body's natural GLP-1 story, and it is one of the best tools for managing hunger when the medication dose drops.
>
> • Sleep that stays consistent. A stable wake time within about sixty minutes most days, with seven to nine hours of opportunity. Hunger follows sleep disruption like a shadow.
>
> • A meal structure that survives real life. Two to three default breakfasts, lunches, and dinners. A planned snack for high-risk times. Planned beats improvised when hunger returns.
>
> • A restart threshold, agreed in advance. Before you taper, decide with your clinician what would trigger restarting, for example, regain of more than five percent of body weight or an A1c that crosses a certain line. This is not a failure plan. It is a safety net.
>
> If those six things are solid and your weight and labs have been stable for at least three months, a slow taper, dose reduced in steps over eight to twelve weeks, with close follow-up, becomes reasonable. If they are not solid, the medication is still doing its job, and staying on is not failure. It is treating a chronic condition with the tools that work.
>
> **What the drugs reveal about us**
>
> The story of GLP-1 and GIP is, on one level, a story about clever pharmacology. Scientists took tiny gut hormones, extended their lifespan, combined them, and changed the way the body handles food.
>
> On another level, it is a mirror. These drugs make it unmistakably clear that appetite and weight are deeply biological. When we raise GLP-1 signaling, people who were told for years they just needed more willpower suddenly find they can walk past a plate of cookies without a fight. When the drug is removed, the old patterns try to return.
>
> That does not mean personal choice doesn't matter. It means choice has always been operating inside a system that was tilted.
>
> GLP-1 drugs can level that system. They can lower food noise in a world that never stops selling snacks. They can buy time for the heart and liver. They can make room for new habits. They cannot fix the food environment on their own. They cannot heal an eating disorder without serious therapy. They cannot build muscle for you.
>

> • Vascular dementia: Slowed thinking and planning problems following strokes or reduced blood flow to the brain

> • Parkinson's disease dementia: Memory and thinking problems that develop after years of Parkinson's.
> • More rare dementias: Conditions like CJD, PSP, and Huntington's-related cognitive decline.

>
> A patient came to see me after more than ten years of suffering. They had seen multiple psychiatrists, done several rounds of therapy, completed Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) for treatment-resistant depression, and tried more than ten antidepressants.
>
> Nothing worked. Or more accurately, the same pattern kept happening: they would start a new medication and feel a little better for a few days. Then they would crash. > **The 10 Types of Depression I Treat**
>
> 1. Major Depressive Disorder
>
> This is what most people picture when they hear the word "depression." It is more than feeling sad for a few days. It can affect mood, energy, sleep, appetite, concentration, and motivation. This is what I experienced during my first year as a resident doctor.
>
> Common symptoms include feeling depressed most of the day, losing interest in things you usually enjoy, sleeping too much or too little, eating much more or much less than usual, feeling exhausted, feeling worthless or guilty, trouble concentrating, and thoughts of death.
>
> How I see it present: People often describe feeling heavy, empty, foggy, numb, or disconnected from themselves. Treatment often includes therapy, medications like SSRIs, SNRIs or wellbutrin, and lifestyle changes. And I have to give a special shoutout to the three GOATs: exercise, nutrition, and sleep. They are not a cure-all, but they matter a lot.
>
> 2. Postpartum Depression
>
> Postpartum depression is depression that happens during pregnancy or after childbirth. It is far more serious than most people realize. This is not the same thing as being tired because you have a newborn, and it is not the same thing as "baby blues."
>
> Symptoms can include persistent sadness, crying often, feeling numb or disconnected, trouble bonding with the baby, severe anxiety, guilt or shame, feeling like a bad mother, and intrusive or scary thoughts.
>
> How I see it present: Many new moms feel pressure to look happy, grateful, and capable, so they suffer quietly. They may think, "I should be enjoying this. What is wrong with me?" Nothing is "wrong" with them. They are dealing with a real medical condition.
>
> Treatment can include therapy, medication, sleep support, family support, and sometimes more urgent psychiatric care.
>
> 3. Seasonal Affective Disorder
>
> Seasonal Affective Disorder, often called S.A.D, is depression that follows a seasonal pattern. For most people, it shows up in the fall or winter when there is less sunlight.
>
> Symptoms often include low mood, low energy, sleeping more than usual, eating more than usual, craving carbohydrates, social withdrawal, and feeling better when spring or summer returns.
>
> How I see it present: People often say, "This happens to me every winter." One of the best treatments is actually bright light therapy. For my patients, I usually recommend a 10,000 lux light box for about 20 to 30 minutes in the morning. I also often recommend morning sunlight, outdoor exercise, and consistent sleep. If it's severe enough, I will offer medication - Wellbutrin can be especially helpful for some people with seasonal depression.
>
> 4. Bipolar Depression
>
> This is the one from the story above. Bipolar depression is depression that happens in someone who also has episodes of mania or hypomania.
>
> The tricky part is that most people do not come to a psychiatrist saying, "I think I have hypomania." They come in when they are depressed. The hypomanic periods may feel productive, confident, energized, and creative.
>
> Symptoms of hypomania can include needing much less sleep but still feeling energized, talking faster than usual, racing thoughts, feeling unusually confident, taking on lots of projects, spending more money than usual, and being more impulsive.
>
> How I see it present: Patients with Bipolar Type 2 often seek help only during the depressive episodes. They may not mention the "up" periods because those periods do not feel like a problem.
>
> Treatment is different from regular major depression. Mood stabilizers like lamotrigine are often used. Antidepressants alone are usually not the right approach and can sometimes make mood less stable.
>
> **Could This Be You Or Someone You Love?**
>
> If you have been struggling with depression, anxiety, mood swings, burnout, or medication after medication that never seems to work, it may be time for a more careful evaluation.
>
> Not a rushed appointment. Not a fifteen-minute checklist. A real psychiatric evaluation from a board-certified psychiatrist that looks at the full picture.
>

>
> 5. PMDD
>
> PMDD stands for Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder. It is not "just PMS." It is a severe mood disorder connected to the menstrual cycle.
>
> The key feature is the pattern. Symptoms show up before the period and improve within a few days after the period starts.
>
> Symptoms can include severe irritability, mood swings, anxiety, depression, feeling overwhelmed, anger, crying spells, sleep changes, and food cravings.
>
> How I see it present: People often say, "I feel like a different person before my period." Then once their period starts, they feel like themselves again.
>
> PMDD is often misdiagnosed as regular depression or anxiety. Treatment can include SSRIs, hormonal contraception, exercise, stress management, and lifestyle support.
>
> 6. Persistent Depressive Disorder
>
> This used to be called dysthymia. It is a chronic, lower-grade form of depression that lasts for years (at least 2 years technically). It may not always look as severe from the outside, but it can be incredibly painful over time.
>
> Symptoms can include low mood most days, low energy, poor self-esteem, trouble sleeping, appetite changes, trouble concentrating, and feeling hopeless.
>
> How I see it present: People often say things like, "I think this is just my personality," "I've always been this way," or "I don't even remember what it feels like to feel normal."
>
> That is what makes it so easy to miss. If someone has felt depressed for years, they may stop recognizing it as depression. I find that therapy can be particularly helpful for people who experience PDD.
>
> 7. Anxious Depression
>
> This is depression mixed with a lot of anxiety, and it is extremely common. Some people with depression do not just feel sad or numb. They feel tense, restless, panicky, on edge, or like something terrible is about to happen.
>
> Symptoms can include constant worry, feeling keyed up or tense, restlessness, trouble concentrating because of worry, fear that something bad will happen, feeling out of control, and trouble sleeping.
>
> How I see it present: The anxiety can be so loud that the depression gets missed. Someone may think they "just have anxiety," when underneath it there is also significant depression.
>
> Treatment needs to address both sides - depression and anxiety. I am usually cautious to prescribe any stimulating medications like Wellbutrin in these cases because, for some people, it can make anxiety worse.
>
> 8. Atypical Depression
>
> The name is confusing because atypical depression is actually not that rare. One of the key features is that the person can feel better temporarily when something good happens. Because of that, people sometimes question whether they are "really depressed."
>
> Symptoms can include mood improving temporarily with positive events, sleeping too much, increased appetite or weight gain, heavy feelings in the arms or legs, and strong sensitivity to rejection or criticism.
>
> How I see it present: People may say, "I can still have a good day sometimes, so maybe I'm not actually depressed." But being able to smile, laugh, or have a good moment does not mean someone is not depressed.
>
> Atypical depression can be very painful, especially because rejection sensitivity can make relationships feel overwhelming.
>
> 9. Melancholic Depression
>
> Melancholic depression is often a more severe and physically intense form of depression. This is the type where people often cannot feel better, even briefly, when something good happens.
>
> Symptoms can include loss of pleasure in almost everything, no mood improvement even when good things happen, waking up very early, depression that feels worse in the morning, moving or speaking more slowly, significant weight loss, excessive guilt, and a deep sense of emptiness.
>
> How I see it present: People often describe this as more than sadness. They may say, "I feel empty," "I feel like my body is made of lead," or "I wake up every morning with this awful feeling."
>
> Treatment often includes antidepressants, close monitoring, and sometimes more intensive treatment depending on severity.
>
> 10. Depression With Psychotic Features
>
> This is one of the most severe forms of depression. It happens when someone has major depression along with psychosis.
>
> Psychosis can include hallucinations or delusions. Hallucinations are seeing or hearing things that are not there. Delusions are fixed beliefs that are not based in reality.
>
> In depression with psychotic features, the beliefs often match the depression. For example, someone may believe, "I am being punished," "I ruined everything," "My family would be better off without me," or "I committed some terrible sin."
>
> How I see it present: This can sometimes be mistaken for schizophrenia. But in depression with psychotic features, the psychosis is tied to the depressive episode.
>
> This condition is serious and usually requires urgent psychiatric care. Treatment often includes an antidepressant plus an antipsychotic medication, and sometimes hospitalization if safety is a concern. This is not something to wait on.
>

> **The 10 Biggest Brain Health Takeaways of 2025**
>
> 1. You do NOT need 10,000 steps a day to protect your brain
>
> One of the most practical findings this year came from a large meta-analysis showing meaningful reductions in dementia, mortality, and depression risk at step counts well below 10,000. For many outcomes, benefits plateaued around 5,000–7,000 steps per day.
> Takeaway: If 10,000 feels overwhelming, fewer steps still meaningfully move the needle.
>
> 2. Creatine entered serious brain-health conversations
>
> Creatine (yes, the powder traditionally consumed by weightlifters) gained traction not just for muscle, but for brain energy metabolism and cognitive resilience, particularly under stress and sleep deprivation. Human data remain early but promising.
> Takeaway: Too soon for blanket recommendations, but this is an area worth watching closely.
>
> 3. There is likely no safe level of alcohol for dementia risk
>
> A major analysis combining observational data with genetic methods found NO protective effect of moderate drinking. None. Zilch. Zero. Dementia risk increased steadily with higher alcohol exposure, without a safe lower threshold.
> Takeaway: From a brain perspective, less alcohol is better.
>
> 4. Lithium quietly emerged as a fascinating Alzheimer's signal
>
> Human brain tissue and animal studies showed lower endogenous lithium levels in people with mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's disease. In mice, lithium depletion worsened pathology, while certain formulations improved memory.
> Takeaway: Lithium's role in brain aging MAY be more important than we realized. Importantly, prescription lithium and over-the-counter lithium orotate are very different. I break this down in a recent Ask a Psychiatrist.
>
> 5. The shingles vaccine–dementia finding surprised many
>
> Population data suggested that people with dementia who received a shingles vaccine had fewer dementia-related deaths and fewer mild cognitive impairment diagnoses over long follow-up. Importantly the studied vaccine in this case was an older version of the vaccine (most countries use an updated version of the vaccine now). This is a promising signal linking immune health and brain aging.
> Takeaway: The immune system and brain aging appear more connected than we once thought.
>
> 6. Metabolic health strongly predicted young-onset dementia risk
>
> A massive cohort study linked metabolic syndrome (a cluster of biochemical and physiological abnormalities associated with the development of cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes) in midlife to significantly higher risk of dementia before age 65, including Alzheimer's and vascular dementia.
> Takeaway: Brain health prevention clearly starts in early/midlife.
>
> 7. GLP-1 medications kept showing up in brain research
>
> Across animal models, observational studies, and early trials, GLP-1 receptor agonists (think Ozempic) were linked to reduced neuroinflammation, lower dementia risk signals, and possible effects on cravings and addiction behaviors.
> Takeaway: Metabolism, inflammation, addiction, and brain health are deeply interconnected. Still too early to prescribe for brain health alone.
>
> 8. Exercise science became more forgiving
>
> More evidence reinforced that concentrating exercise into one or two days per week (like on the weekend) can still produce meaningful health and brain benefits.
> Takeaway: Consistency matters, but perfection is not required.
>
> 9. Chronic insomnia appeared to accelerate brain aging
>
> Large prospective studies linked chronic insomnia to higher risk of mild cognitive impairment and dementia, along with markers of faster brain aging.
> Takeaway: Persistent insomnia deserves evaluation, not normalization.
>
> 10. Many memory concerns still come from reversible causes
>
> Across clinic visits and research, a consistent theme emerged: many cognitive complaints are driven by treatable, reversible factors, not dementia.
> Takeaway: Before assuming the worst, rule out reversible causes first. Depression, anxiety, ADHD, hypothyroidism, etc. This is exactly what I focus on in Goodman Memory Clinic and in 1:1 evaluations.

> Now, do we need improved education around deprescribing? Yes. Better education around safely coming off medications is a conversation worth having. Not just for SSRIs, but for many medications. I actually spent the last couple weeks writing about exactly this — the concept of deprescribing. You can find those newsletters in my recent posts. But that is a very different conversation from claiming SSRIs are harder to quit than heroin. One is a legitimate policy discussion. The other is misinformation that puts lives at risk.
>
> **Why This Is So Dangerous**
>
> There are legitimate conversations to be had about overprescribing. About whether every person on an SSRI truly needs one. About the importance of therapy and lifestyle alongside medication. I have these conversations every day in my clinic.
>
> But what RFK said crosses a different line entirely.
>
> When the Secretary of Health and Human Services tells the American public that antidepressants are harder to quit than heroin, people stop taking their medication.
>
> Not because their doctor told them to. Not because they worked with a psychiatrist to taper safely. But because they are scared. Because a powerful government official told them their medication is as dangerous as one of the most destructive drugs in human history.
>
> And depression is not something you can stop treating abruptly without consequences.
>
> I have seen what untreated depression does. The cognitive decline. The isolation. The inability to work or care for family. The hospitalizations. In the worst cases, the loss of life. The medications (SSRI's) being vilified right now are some of the most effective tools we have to prevent those outcomes.
>
> **What SSRIs Actually Treat**
>
> In the middle of all this noise, I think it is worth pausing to remember what these medications actually do.
>
> SSRIs are helpful for treating:
>
> Major depressive disorder
>
> Generalized anxiety disorder
>
> Obsessive compulsive disorder
>
> Panic disorder
>
> Post-traumatic stress disorder
>
> Social anxiety disorder
>
> Premenstrual dysphoric disorder
>
> Bulimia nervosa
>
> These are not rare conditions. They are among the most common and most debilitating conditions in the world. And for many people — not all, but many — SSRIs provide meaningful, sometimes life-changing relief when therapy and lifestyle changes alone are not enough.
>
> Are they perfect? No. Side effects are real. They do not work for everyone. Coming off them requires care and proper guidance.
>
> But they are not heroin. That comparison is not a matter of opinion or political perspective. It is factually, medically, scientifically wrong. And saying it from one of the most powerful health platforms in the country puts real people at real risk.
>
> **If You Are on an SSRI and Feeling Unsure Right Now**

>

> Sunday Scaries
>
> I used to experience them all the time.
>
> That pit-in-your-stomach feeling. The irritability. The mental spiral about Monday. The sense that you couldn't fully enjoy the present moment because the week was already looming.
>
> I felt that way for years throughout medical school and residency, until I built a simple Sunday routine that changed everything.
>
> Even now, after finishing residency, running my dream practices, and having much more control over my schedule, I still occasionally feel a blip of Sunday scaries.
>
> When I do, I tap back into this routine, and things fall back into place.
>
> If you've ever felt that familiar knot in your stomach on Sunday night, you're not alone.
>
> The Sunday scaries are not a personal failure or a sign you're doing life wrong. From a psychiatric standpoint, they're a form of anticipatory anxiety, meaning anxiety about the week ahead.
>
> In plain terms, they show up as dread, restlessness, irritability, trouble relaxing, or difficulty sleeping as the weekend comes to an end. I see this constantly in my clinic.
>
> So whether you're reading this Sunday night after the Super Bowl, Monday morning, or sometime later, save this as your Sunday Scaries Prevention Guide.
>
> Nothing fancy. Just structure that helps calm the nervous system.
>
> **A quick hello for new readers**
>
> There are a lot of new faces here, so a brief intro.
>
> I'm Dr. Jake Goodman, a board-certified psychiatrist focused on brain health
>
> You can learn from or work with me in a few ways:
>
> • This free Sunday newsletter
> • My paid Wednesday newsletter with deeper dives on brain health and memory
> • Goodman Memory Clinic, my concierge virtual clinic focused on memory and dementia-risk reduction
> • Goodman Psychiatry, my virtual mental health practice for high-achieving professionals (ADHD, depression, anxiety, trauma, burnout, and more)
>
> Okay. Let's dive in.
>
> **1. I meal prep breakfast, not dinner**
>
> Yes, you read that right.
>
> Most people think about meal prepping dinners. And if you do that on Sundays, you're crushing it. I try to as well, but breakfast prep is faster, easier, and more impactful for me.
>
> Breakfast sets the tone for the entire day.
>
> Every Sunday, I make a high-protein overnight oats recipe that fuels me all morning on Monday and Tuesday with almost zero effort. The whole thing takes less than five minutes.
>
> Here's what I do:
>
> • 2 cups old-fashioned oats
> • 1 and 3/4 cups almond milk (or any milk you like)
> • 1/4 cup kefir or Greek yogurt
>
> I use kefir because it's gentler on my stomach. Both provide probiotics, which support gut health and may indirectly benefit mood and cognition.
>
> Then I add:
>
> • A mix of nuts and seeds (walnuts or pecans, hemp seeds, pumpkin seeds, flax seeds, sesame seeds)
> • 2 big spoonfuls of almond butter
> • A drizzle of honey
>
> I literally made this five minutes ago and snapped a photo before putting it in the fridge. I added a drizzle of cinnamon on top. It may not look amazing yet, but trust me, after it sits overnight, it's incredible.
>
> Mix it together and put it in the fridge.
>
> In the morning, I scoop some into a bowl and add:
>
> • Blueberries, which are rich in anthocyanins linked to brain and vascular health
> • Strawberries, apples, or whatever fruit we have on hand
>
> It's fast, filling, easy to clean up, and removes one decision from my mornings. Based on rough estimates, it's about 15 to 20 grams of protein per serving (3-4 servings in total)
>
> My brain loves it.
>
> **2. I protect my sleep on Sunday night**
>
> Sunday sleep matters more than most people realize.
>
> I aim for seven to eight hours, closer to eight when possible. Here's the rule I live by:
>
> If I want seven and a half hours of sleep, I'm in bed for eight.
> If I want eight hours of sleep, I'm in bed for eight and a half.
>
> Give yourself a 30-minute buffer.
>
> You're not a robot. You can't just flip a switch and turn your brain off. That extra half hour gives you wiggle room if it takes time to fall asleep or if you wake up at 3 a.m. to use the bathroom (me).
>
> Nights like the Super Bowl, Olympics, or other late events make this harder, and that's okay. This is a practice, not a perfection rule. Even giving yourself more time in bed helps reduce sleep-related anxiety.
>
> **3. I do light Monday prep (not "getting ahead")**
>
> I used to treat Sundays like a workday. I try not to anymore.
>
> But I also don't ignore everything and let Monday become a disaster. I focus on removing friction.
>
> I'll spend a little time:
>
> • Reviewing my schedule
> • Prepping a few notes
> • Clearing my desk and decluttering
> • Scheduling a couple of important emails
>
> And yes, also writing this newsletter.
>
> I run two clinical practices, and this small amount of prep dramatically reduces the "everything is on fire" feeling on Monday morning. This works in almost any job.
>
> **4. I connect with people**
>
> This one is key. Anxiety thrives in isolation.
>
> On Sundays, I intentionally connect with family and friends. Today, I talked with my parents, caught up with a friend from residency, and spent quality time with Leo—mostly giggling, burping, and making ridiculous fart noises with my mouth, which he found absolutely hilarious.
>
> Social connection is one of the most reliable ways to calm anticipatory anxiety, and it's often the first thing people drop when they're stressed.
>
> **5. I move my body, usually hard**
>
> Sunday is often a cardio day for me. Soccer, tennis, sprints, or a solid aerobic session.
>
> This helps:
>
> • Reduce stress hormones
> • Improve sleep quality
> • Create a clean emotional reset before Monday
>
> You don't need to do exactly this. But movement matters.
>
> **Practical ways to reduce Sunday scaries**
>
> A few simple strategies I recommend often:
>
> Clean your workspace. A messy desk is a visual stressor.
>
> Lay out clothes for Monday.
>
> Limit screens Sunday night, especially work email. Checking emails late at night is one of the fastest ways to worsen Sunday scaries.
>
> Create a Sunday ritual. Family dinner, a show, a walk. Something predictable and grounding.
>
> **One important reflection**
>
> Sometimes Sunday scaries aren't about anxiety management at all.
>
> Sometimes they're about job dissatisfaction.
>
> In my clinic, I see some healthcare workers and high achievers who are exhausted because they're in environments that are chronically unsupportive, unsustainable, or toxic.
>
> Sometimes the work is learning how to cope.
> Sometimes the work is planning an exit.
> And sometimes it's both.
>
> If your dread feels constant, it may be worth gently asking yourself:
>
> Is this stress, or is this misalignment?
>
> If Sunday nights feel hard, you're not broken.
> Your nervous system is responding to what's ahead.
>
> A little structure, connection, and self-compassion go a long way.
